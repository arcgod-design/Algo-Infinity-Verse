Burrows-Wheeler Transform

BWT + Move-to-Front + Run-Length Encoding

The Burrows-Wheeler Transform doesn't compress data—it rearranges it. By sorting all cyclic rotations of a string, it clusters identical characters together. When this perfectly primed string is passed through a Move-to-Front (MTF) encoder, it produces massive runs of zeros. Finally, a simple Run-Length Encoding (RLE) squashes those zeros, resulting in the legendary compression powering bzip2.

Theoretical Deep Dive

🌪️

Why BWT Clusters Data

The BWT sorts all cyclic rotations of a string lexicographically. Because english text has predictable patterns (e.g., 'T' often precedes 'H', 'E' often precedes 'R'), sorting by the prefix naturally groups similar succeeding characters together in the last column. This turns a high-entropy string into a low-entropy string with lots of repeated characters.

🎯

Move-to-Front's Magic

MTF takes a string with clustered characters and turns it into a sequence of small numbers. Whenever it outputs a character, it moves that character to the front of its alphabet list (index 0). If the same character appears 5 times in a row, MTF outputs index, 0, 0, 0, 0. This perfectly converts character runs into runs of zeros.

🗜️

Run-Length Encoding

By itself, RLE on english text is terrible because characters rarely repeat. But after BWT and MTF, our data is now primarily long strings of zeros! RLE squashes these zeros into tiny `(0, count)` pairs, resulting in massive final compression ratios. This exact three-step pipeline is the heart of the famous bzip2 compressor.
